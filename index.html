How I explain things

I learn by reading and working examples, so I teach the same way. Every idea below goes through four steps, and the colors stay the same all page.

  1. Say the idea in plain words. No jargon until the picture is clear.
  2. Work one small example by hand, with real numbers, so nothing is hidden inside a library call.
  3. Show where it breaks. Most mistakes in machine learning look like success at first.
  4. Hand over an exercise. The answer stays folded away until you have tried it.

The five projects are in the order I would teach their ideas: get clean data, judge a model honestly, put it where people can use it, compare many models fairly, then try something that does not have a known answer yet.

Lesson 1: a model is only as good as its data

Football Intelligence Dataset

I scraped, merged, cleaned and audited six seasons of player statistics from Europe's Big 5 leagues, with no API and no ready-made download.

  • 21,100 player-season rows
  • 109 raw merged columns
  • 30 CSV files scraped
  • 6 seasons, 2020 to 2026

What I did

Scrape. Selenium with undetected-chromedriver pulled the five stat tables for each season. FBref's tables have two header rows, so I flattened them into single column names. Random delays kept the scraping polite.

Merge and clean. I outer-joined the tables on Player, Squad and Born, then stacked the seasons. I fixed ages stored in a "YY-DDD" format, dropped duplicate columns, and dropped rows that were 75% or more empty.

Audit. Some gaps could be filled by rule, for example Save% from saves and shots. A 0/0 case is a structural zero, not a missing value. Missing Born and Nation values were filled by manual research. I pruned features whose correlation was above 0.90.

Test. I checked for duplicate Player-Squad-Season rows, drew null heatmaps, and calculated the missing percentage per row. The checks caught impossible values such as a Clean Sheet % above 100, and I spot-checked well-known players against what I knew.

# merge 5 stat tables per season, then stack all seasons
base = standard.merge(shooting, on=["Player", "Squad", "Born"], how="outer")
final_df = pd.concat(merged_dfs.values(), ignore_index=True)
# one player, every season of his career, all stats attached

Concept. An outer join keeps a row even when the other table has no match for it. An inner join would quietly throw those players away.

Trap. If the join key is not unique, rows multiply without any error. Two players can share a name, so I joined on name, club and birth year together.

Try it. Make two tiny tables of five players each, with two players sharing a name. Merge on name only, then on name plus birth year. How many rows does each give?

Show the answer

Merging on name only gives 7 rows instead of 5. The shared name appears twice in each table, so it matches 2 × 2 = 4 times instead of 2, and the other three names give one row each. Adding birth year makes every key unique and the count goes back to 5.

Why it exists: football still runs on intuition and reputation. This dataset is a first step toward making statistics usable by any fan, analyst or small club, not only those with private data teams.

Lesson 2: accuracy can mislead you

Telco customer churn

Telecom companies lose roughly 15 to 25 percent of customers every year, and winning a new customer costs far more than keeping one. The task: find the customers most likely to leave, so the company can reach them first.

  • 7,043 customers
  • 21 columns
  • 3 models compared
  • 26.5% of customers churned

Reading the data before modelling

This is a classification problem, because the answer is a category (leaves or stays), not a number. Churn is imbalanced: 5,174 customers stayed and 1,869 left.

Hidden missing values. The data looked complete. But TotalCharges held blank spaces for 11 customers, all with tenure 0, and a blank is text, not a null. Converting the column to numbers exposed them as true missing values. With 11 rows out of 7,043 I removed them.

Encoding. Two-answer columns became 0 and 1. Columns with three or more answers, such as contract type, were one-hot encoded. Numbering "month-to-month, one year, two year" as 1, 2, 3 would tell the model that two years is three times month-to-month, which is not true.

Scaling. Tenure, monthly charges and total charges were standardized, so a column measured in hundreds does not outweigh one measured in tens just because its numbers are bigger.

Splitting. A stratified split held out 20% as a test set (1,407 customers) and kept the 73/27 ratio in both parts. The random seed is fixed, so the same customers land in the same sets each run.

What the data said

Churners are newer customers: their median tenure was 10 months against about 38 for loyal ones. Almost all churn happens on month-to-month contracts. Fiber optic service and electronic-check payment stood out as the biggest warning signs. K-means with the elbow method suggested three customer segments, which I drew with PCA.

Three models, one honest comparison

I trained K-nearest neighbors (k = 11), logistic regression with balanced class weights, and a small PyTorch neural network (64 and 32 hidden units, Adam, 100 epochs). For KNN and the network I lowered the decision threshold to 0.30, so they flag a customer sooner. That trades precision for recall on purpose.

Concept. Precision asks: of the customers I flagged, how many really left? Recall asks: of the customers who really left, how many did I flag? Moving the threshold trades one for the other. Try it on the board at the top of this page.

Trap. A model that says "stays" for everyone is 73.5% accurate here (5,174 of 7,043) and catches no churner at all. A score of 75% sounds fine until you know that.

Try it. There are 1,000 customers and 265 of them will churn. A model flags 200 customers and 120 of those really churn. Work out its precision, its recall and its accuracy, then compare the accuracy with "always say stays".

Show the answer

Precision = 120 / 200 = 60%. Recall = 120 / 265 = 45.3%. The model made 80 false alarms and missed 145 churners, so the true negatives are 735 minus 80 = 655. Accuracy = (120 + 655) / 1000 = 77.5%. "Always say stays" scores 73.5%. The model gains 4 points of accuracy but still misses more than half of the churners.

What was hard

The imbalance pulled every model toward "stays", so I had to stop trusting accuracy and tune thresholds and class weights instead. I also had to accept a precision near 50%, which means many false alarms, in return for recall above 75%. The neural network added complexity and did not beat the simpler model on the business goal.

Lesson 3: a model nobody can use is not finished

Football position predictor

A Random Forest model that guesses a player's position from five numbers, deployed as a web app anyone can open.

You enter age, minutes played, goals, assists and tackles. The app returns a position label. The label can be a single position or a pair such as MF,FW, the way football data sites list players who play in two roles.

Open on Hugging Face

Concept. A random forest is many decision trees, each trained on a slightly different sample of the data. Every tree votes and the majority wins. One tree memorizes quirks, but a crowd of different trees averages them out.

Trap. Five inputs give the model a thin picture. A defensive midfielder and a centre-back can post similar numbers, so predictions for borderline players are educated guesses. A confident-looking answer is not the same as a correct one.

Try it. Before you press Submit, write down the position you expect for a player with 3,000 minutes, 0 goals, 1 assist and 120 tackles. Then run it, and then change one number at a time to see which input moves the answer most.

Lesson 1's dataset was built for this kind of task: position classification from player-season statistics.

Lesson 4: compare fairly, then question the winner

DeepSight: political bias in news

Can a model tell whether a news article leans left, center or right? DeepSight puts three generations of text classifiers through one identical pipeline so their scores can be compared directly.

A paper written with Zawad Ishmam Hriddo, Department of CSE, BRAC University.

  • 16,951 articles after cleaning
  • 3 classes: left, center, right
  • 13,560 / 3,391 train and test

Setup that keeps the comparison fair

Everything that learns from data, including the TF-IDF vectorizer and the Word2Vec embeddings, was fitted on the training set only, so the test set never leaks in. The classes are imbalanced (left 44.96%, right 31.67%, center 23.36%), so Macro F1, which weighs each class equally, is the main score.

What the results taught me

A simple baseline is hard to beat. TF-IDF with logistic regression reached 73.99% accuracy, ahead of every plain RNN. The best plain RNN reached 68.09%.

Gates matter for long text. Swapping the RNN for an LSTM lifted accuracy to 72.78%. Keeping the Word2Vec embeddings frozen, so training did not distort them, reached 77.29% and a Macro F1 of 0.7667, the best of all the recurrent models.

What you feed a Transformer matters. Articles are longer than the 512-token window. RoBERTa on the first 512 tokens scored 73.55%. Keeping the first 128 and the last 382 tokens raised it to 77.50%, so the end of an article carries signal too.

The cost. The frozen LSTM took about 2,182 seconds to train, RoBERTa about 2,944 and DeBERTa-v3 about 6,609. Logistic regression trains in a small fraction of that.

Concept. Last-state pooling asks a recurrent network to squeeze a whole article into its final hidden state. Mean pooling lets every word contribute. In my runs the last-state model fell to 43.11% accuracy, so it learned almost nothing.

Trap, and why I do not trust the winner yet. DeBERTa-v3 reached 93.78% accuracy and 0.9362 Macro F1, a 16-point jump over RoBERTa. That deserves suspicion before applause:

Most neural results come from a single run, with no confidence intervals. The split is random at article level, so it measures accuracy on familiar publishers, not on outlets the model has never seen. Training accuracy hit 99.9% while validation loss rose after the third epoch.

My next steps from the paper: repeat DeBERTa and RoBERTa with several seeds, add near-duplicate filtering, finish a chunked long-document Transformer, and report confidence intervals.

Try it. On this data the largest class is about 45%. A model scores 43%. Is it learning something?

Show the answer

No. It does worse than always guessing "left". That was the last-state RNN. The paper does not measure gradients, so vanishing gradients is a likely cause but still a hypothesis, not a finding.

Lesson 5: research does not come with an answer key

Teachable object recognizer

Prototype. A demo of my ongoing thesis on the same topic, not the finished system.

Imagine you are blind and want your phone to know your own mug, your own medicine bottle, your own keys. The prototype lets a person teach it an object with 10 to 20 photos and a name, and recognize that object later.

According to the app, it uses CLIP ViT-B/32 embeddings, MediaPipe hand-centric cropping, and the TEgO dataset.

Concept. An embedding turns a photo into a long list of numbers, so that photos of similar things get similar lists. Teaching an object means storing the lists from its photos. Recognizing means comparing a new photo's list with the stored ones. This is why a handful of photos can be enough and nothing has to be retrained.

Hand-anchoring is my thesis idea in miniature: the object a person holds is the object they care about, so the image is cropped around the hand. When no hand is found, the app falls back to the whole image.

Trap. A confidence of 0.939 on a photo taken in the same room as the teaching photos is an easy test. It says little about how the system behaves with a different background, different light, or an object that looks like another. Evaluating this properly is part of the thesis.

Try it. Teach it one object with 10 photos from different angles and rooms. Then test it with photos in a completely new place. How does the confidence change, and when does it start to confuse similar objects?
